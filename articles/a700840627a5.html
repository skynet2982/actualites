To Speed Up Deportation of Afghans, Paris Could Give the Taliban Control of the Embassy

Margaux Benn

Voir l'article original

Western countries are accelerating the deportation of Afghans, sometimes in exchange for diplomatic concessions to a theocracy they do not officially recognize.

Given how deadly the Taliban regime is, such a pact would violate international law, France’s diplomatic stance and ethical principles. It would endanger the lives of countless Afghan families. Two sources familiar with the matter told Le Figaro in early November that Paris is on the verge of concluding an agreement with the Taliban whereby the regime would agree to take in Afghan nationals that Paris wishes to deport in exchange for the recognition of Taliban diplomats who would take control of the Afghan embassy in Paris.

In early October, Agence France-Presse first reported that France was holding “technical discussions” — later publicly confirmed by the French Ministry of Foreign Affairs — on the deportation of Afghans residing in France illegally who pose “a risk” to the safety of French citizens. France is home to approximately 100,000 Afghan refugees, making them one of the country’s largest refugee communities. Paris has already deported several Afghans, but these talks aim to increase the number of deportations, according to sources.

“What about the rule of law?” said Vincent Beaugrand, director of the France Terre d’Asile migrants rights group. “The principle of non-refoulement is an absolute and non-negotiable principle that protects against indirect capital punishment. It is an international legal obligation, which the UN, incidentally, reminded European states of just a few weeks ago. No reason could justify deportation to a territory where such a risk exists.”

How can the government be assured that individuals sent back to Afghanistan do not face death, torture or imprisonment? “That depends on the good faith of the local authorities,” a source in Paris said. In other words, the Taliban.

Pressure on diplomats in Paris

In late October, Intelligence Online revealed that Taliban emissaries are increasing pressure on Afghan diplomats from the former republic who are still stationed at the Afghan embassy in Paris, urging them to join the team the Taliban intend to install at the embassy — and to travel throughout Europe to expand its diplomatic and consular network. In France, where most of the staff has already left the embassy due to unpaid salaries, a well-informed official said that the Taliban have not even attempted to recruit anyone. “They have appointed their own people, likely members of their intelligence services,” he said.

Since the Taliban returned power in Afghanistan on August 15, 2021, certain Afghan diplomatic missions, including in Paris, have refused to align themselves with the regime, which has cut off all funding to them. The Paris embassy is therefore recognized by France, but consular affairs (such as visa applications) are handled directly by the authorities in Kabul. Ambassador Mohammad Homayoun Azizi, who has been in office since 2020, still lives in the beautiful mansion, which serves as both embassy and residence, on a tree-lined avenue in the 16th arrondissement, but he is, in fact, powerless. His presence, however, is highly symbolic, and he continues to engage in official dialogue with his European counterparts through frequent videoconferences.

Le Figaro was able to confirm the link between these two developments: The discussions condition the expulsion of Afghan nationals on the recognition of Taliban diplomats at the embassy in Paris.

“Nothing has been confirmed”

“For the time being, nothing has been confirmed yet,” officials in Paris said. But according to the weekly newspaper Le Canard Enchaîné, the French foreign ministry “has revoked the accreditations of Azizi and his team, who were forced to cease their consular activities on October 9.” And, according to a source familiar with the matter, the Taliban have already selected replacements for the current ambassador and other embassy staff members.

There is only one precedent for this type of agreement in Europe: Since summer 2025, the embassy in Berlin and the consulates in Bonn and Munich, Germany, have been run by Taliban representatives. Their primary objective is to facilitate the return to Afghanistan of undocumented individuals Germany wishes to deport. The official line maintains that these individuals had committed crimes in Germany. The reality, it seems, is quite different: According to several German NGOs, not all of them had been convicted of a criminal offense.

Beyond the moral and legal issues, the presence of Taliban diplomats in Europe is highly dangerous for the Afghans concerned. Intelligence Online notes that in Germany, some 35,000 Afghans admitted under humanitarian programs must renew their passports with these diplomatic missions to extend their visas. The process requires them to provide the new authorities with information about their families who remain in Afghanistan. For the relatives of exiled dissidents, that could amount to a death sentence.

“France is the country of human rights; it is a country that holds a seat on the UN Security Council. If Paris were to conclude such an agreement with a regime it does not even recognize, it would send a shameful message,” a source familiar with the matter said. The only two remaining employees at the Afghan embassy in Paris are women. If France were to tolerate — and therefore effectively recognize — a Taliban embassy in Paris, it would consent to the implementation, on its territory, of the regime’s policy in Afghanistan: excluding women and representatives of the former republic in favor of the Taliban.

“As for feminist diplomacy, we will have to wait and see,” sources said sarcastically, referring to the doctrine French President Emmanuel Macron announced in 2019.

A Taliban delegation in Brussels

On June 23, thanks to a special 24-hour visa, a Taliban delegation visited the European Commission in Brussels to hold discussions aimed at enabling member states to resume deportations to Afghanistan. That followed an initial meeting held in Kabul in January. Several members of the delegation, subject to International Criminal Court (ICC) arrest warrants, were able to travel without facing legal repercussions. In early November, a European diplomat told Le Figaro that the Brussels meeting had in no way determined or accelerated the decision to reach an agreement allowing for the deportation of Afghans from France. At the time, the Taliban had boasted on social media that the opposite was true.

On October 30, The New York Times reported that the Trump administration was on the verge of deporting 150 Afghans, including relatives of people who had worked with U.S. forces, as well as a gay man.

When the Taliban returned to power in August 2021, people wondered how long their self-sufficient regime — cut off from the global community (and the global financial system), or at least the Western world, and under international sanctions — could last. A year and a half later, the first Afghans were deported from Europe back to their country. It took less than five years for Germany to reach an agreement with the Taliban, and for France to appear ready to follow suit.
